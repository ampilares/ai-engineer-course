This is the finish line: a production capstone that proves you can turn a probabilistic model into a system someone could actually depend on, and defend every decision in it. It's where all eighteen modules converge into one artifact.

## What the capstone must be

Not a demo. A **working system** that solves a real (or realistically scoped) problem end to end, exhibiting the whole discipline:

- **A genuine problem** with a defined user and a measurable goal: ideally the process you baselined in Module 13.
- **Real engineering**: typed, tested, reproducible; data in and out of storage; the failure paths handled (Module 3).
- **Justified AI**: a model used only where ambiguity earns it, wrapped in schemas, validation, and confidence routing (Modules 8, 10); simpler alternatives considered and, where they won, chosen.
- **An eval suite**: a representative dataset with graders, run as a gate, reporting honest numbers including failures (Module 14).
- **Safety**: a threat model, least privilege, human approval on consequential actions, data minimization, and red-team findings turned into eval cases (Module 15).
- **Operations**: deployed with monitoring, a cost/latency budget and SLOs, a runbook, and rollback (Module 16).
- **A business case**: the manual baseline vs. the measured pilot, all costs counted, ROI stated honestly (Module 13).

You don't need all six domains at large scale: you need each *present and coherent*. A small system that is genuinely evaluated, secured, deployed, and measured beats an ambitious one that's a demo in a trench coat.

## Defending your work

Real AI engineering means being able to answer hard questions about your system. Rehearse these (the oral-defense prompts) until your answers are specific and evidence-backed:

- Which component causes the largest share of errors, and *how do you know*? (Your eval breakdown answers this.)
- What happens if the model, vector store, or a downstream API is unavailable?
- Which action has the highest blast radius, and who authorizes it?
- Why is this an LLM problem rather than a database query or a rules engine?
- Which metric could improve while the user experience gets *worse*?
- How would you cut cost by 50% without breaching the quality bar?
- What new data would most improve the system?

If any answer is a shrug, that's not a defense failure: it's your next week of work, precisely located.

## Scope for evidence, not feature count

Choose one user, one workflow, one primary outcome, and one consequential risk. A narrow end-to-end pilot is stronger than a platform with six unfinished features. Freeze a walking skeleton early: representative input enters, a versioned decision occurs, the guarded outcome is recorded, and a trace explains what happened. Add sophistication only when an eval failure justifies it.

Plan milestones around risk retirement: data access and baseline, system contract, first end-to-end path, evaluation dataset, security controls, pilot, reliability exercise, and final decision. Keep a decision log for rejected architectures and failed experiments; they demonstrate judgment when grounded in evidence.

Your defense should state the strongest limitation before reviewers discover it. Show a failure trace, an eval regression caught before release, a cost/quality trade-off, and the human control for residual risk. Separate what the pilot measured from what a broader rollout would still need to prove.

## Guided build: final capstone checklist

Before submission, verify the repository contains reproducible setup, architecture and data-flow diagrams, typed contracts, tests and evals, versioned prompts/configs, threat model, privacy/data register, traces and dashboard, cost/latency envelope, deployment and rollback, incident runbook, pilot evidence, and model/system card.

Run the project from a clean environment and rehearse the demo under one injected failure. The final recommendation must be one of stop, redesign, continue the pilot, or scale, supported by the rubric evidence. Graduation demonstrates that you can make and defend that decision, not that every AI prototype deserves production.
