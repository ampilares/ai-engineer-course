Language models are probability machines; evaluations are statistics; A/B tests are hypothesis tests. If vectors are AI's geometry, probability is its native tongue, and a few core habits of probabilistic thinking will serve you in every module ahead.

## Probability as quantified uncertainty

A probability is a number between 0 and 1 that quantifies uncertainty. It can describe a long-run frequency, such as defects per thousand products, or a degree of belief conditional on current evidence, such as the probability of rain given a forecast. For repeated, comparable cases, `0.85` should mean that the outcome occurs about 85 times in 100. Two disciplines follow:

- **Probabilities of all the options sum to 1.** A model distributing belief over 100,000 possible next tokens is dividing exactly 1.0 of probability among them: boosting one option necessarily shrinks others.
- **Calibration is a quality bar** (you met it in spirit already): a system whose "80% confident" claims are right 80% of the time is calibrated. One whose 80% means "who knows" is decorating its guesses.

## Distributions: the shape of uncertainty

A **distribution** assigns a probability to every possible outcome: picture a bar chart over the options. What matters practically is its *shape*:

- **Peaked** (one bar dominates) → the model strongly favors a small number of outcomes. For many code models, a prompt such as `import numpy as` will strongly favor a continuation containing `np`, although the exact distribution depends on the model and tokenizer.
- **Flat** (many similar bars) → genuine uncertainty. After `Once upon a time,` many continuations are plausible.

You've already manipulated this: Module 7's temperature dial *reshapes* a distribution (sharpening it toward the peak or flattening it toward uniform) without changing what the model knows.

## Conditional probability: the 'given' changes everything

P(rain) and P(rain **given** dark clouds) are different numbers: probability updates as evidence arrives. This "given" is the deepest connective in AI:

- A language model computes P(next token **given** all tokens so far): change the prompt, change the condition, change every probability. *That is mechanically why prompting works.*
- The classic trap is inverting conditions: P(positive test | disease) ≠ P(disease | positive test). When the condition is rare, these diverge wildly: a 99%-accurate test on a 1-in-1000 condition yields mostly false alarms among its positives. You met this exact structure as precision vs. recall in Module 5's threshold widget.

## Samples lie when they're small

Flip a fair coin 10 times and 7 heads is unremarkable; 7,000 in 10,000 means the coin is loaded. **Small samples produce wide uncertainty; larger samples make estimates more stable.** Sample size alone is not enough: biased or unrepresentative data can remain misleading at any scale. This is why Module 5 requires representative test sets, Module 14 requires evaluation sets that cover important segments, and Module 12 asks you to simulate A/B tests before trusting an apparent uplift.

<Callout type="note">
Build a professional reflex: whenever a result looks impressive, ask *how many observations produced it, how representative were they, what is the uncertainty, and what is the base rate?* Those questions expose many misleading AI claims.
</Callout>
