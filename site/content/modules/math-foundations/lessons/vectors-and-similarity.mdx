Four mathematical ideas recur throughout this course: **vectors**, **probability**, **softmax**, and **gradients**. You do not need formal proofs to begin. You do need a practical intuition for what each idea represents, when it is useful, and where its limits appear. We begin with the representation that lets machines compare meaning.

## A vector is just a list of numbers

`[2.1, 0.8]` is a vector. So is a list of 1,536 numbers describing a sentence. Two mental pictures, both useful:

- **A point/arrow in space**: `[2.1, 0.8]` is the arrow from the origin to that spot. With 2 numbers you can draw it; with 1,536 you can't, but the geometry works identically.
- **A row of measurements**: each position measures one property. A customer as `[age, orders, days_since_login]`; a word as hundreds of learned properties no one individually named.

Once values are represented as vectors, geometry provides useful notions of **distance** and **direction** between them.

## Similarity is an angle

The most common measure here is **cosine similarity**, which measures the *angle* between vectors while ignoring their lengths:

- Pointing the same way → cosine ≈ **1** (similar)
- Perpendicular → cosine ≈ **0** (unrelated)
- Opposite → cosine ≈ **−1** (opposed)

The **dot product** is the core operation used to compute it: multiply matching positions, then add the results (`a·b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + …`). When Module 7 compares query and key vectors, and Module 9 retrieves chunks with similar embeddings, the systems use this same arithmetic in many more dimensions.

Cosine similarity is a ranking signal, not a universal measure of correctness. A score such as `0.92` is meaningful only for a particular embedding model, dataset, and task. Choose acceptance thresholds with representative examples instead of importing a threshold from another system.

## Matrices: many vectors, one grip

A **matrix** is a grid of numbers, which you can also view as a stack of vectors. Multiplying a vector by a matrix transforms it by rotating, stretching, or remixing its components into a new vector. Matrix multiplication accounts for much of a neural network's computation, alongside biases, nonlinearities, normalization, routing, and other operations introduced later. You do not need to hand-compute large matrices in this course. You do need to recognize that most model parameters are learned numbers stored in matrices and related tensors.

<Callout type="tip">
**Dimensions beyond three use the same arithmetic.** A 1,536-dimensional embedding contains 1,536 learned coordinates. Individual coordinates are usually not named, independently meaningful properties, but distance, angle, and matrix operations still work as they do in two dimensions.
</Callout>
