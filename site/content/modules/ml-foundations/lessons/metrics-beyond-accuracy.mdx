Here's a fraud detector with 99% accuracy: `return "not fraud"`. If 1% of transactions are fraudulent, always saying "no" is right 99% of the time, and catches nothing. **Accuracy collapses under class imbalance**, and most valuable problems are imbalanced: fraud, churn, defects, rare diseases, security incidents.

## The confusion matrix: four ways to be right or wrong

Every binary prediction lands in one of four cells:

| | predicted positive | predicted negative |
|---|---|---|
| **actually positive** | true positive (TP) | false negative (FN): *missed it* |
| **actually negative** | false positive (FP): *false alarm* | true negative (TN) |

From these, the two metrics that matter:

- **Precision** = TP / (TP + FP): *of my alarms, how many were real?* Low precision = alert fatigue; humans stop trusting the system.
- **Recall** = TP / (TP + FN): *of the real cases, how many did I catch?* Low recall = the bad thing slips through.
- **F1**: the harmonic mean of the two, useful when you must report one number; it punishes lopsidedness.

## The threshold is a business decision

Most classifiers output a score; *you* choose the cutoff. Slide it and watch the trade-off: precision and recall pull in opposite directions:

Notice something important as you drag: **accuracy barely moves while precision and recall swing wildly.** That's accuracy hiding the decision that actually matters.

Where should the threshold sit? Wherever the *costs* say. Cancer screening: a false negative can be fatal, a false positive is a follow-up test ⇒ favor recall. Auto-blocking customer payments: a false positive is an angry customer, a false negative is one missed fraud ⇒ favor precision (and route the gray zone to a human: a pattern you'll reuse in every AI system).

## Beyond binary: a survival kit

- **Regression** (predicting numbers): MAE (mean absolute error: robust, interpretable in units) and RMSE (punishes large misses harder).
- **Ranking** (search, recommendations, retrieval): does the right item appear in the top k? You'll meet *recall@k* and *MRR* again in the RAG module, where they measure whether retrieval found the right document.
- **Calibration**: when the model says "80% likely," is it right about 80% of the time? Crucial whenever scores drive decisions or get shown to humans.

<Callout type="tip">
One metric can improve while the user experience worsens: recall rising as precision quietly craters into alert fatigue, for example. Always look at the pair, and at whom each error lands on. This instinct is half of Module 14.
</Callout>

## Turn metrics into expected cost

Precision and recall become useful when connected to volume and consequences. If 10,000 cases arrive weekly, a threshold with 80% precision and 50% recall does not merely produce two percentages, it determines how many people review false alarms and how many harmful cases remain unseen. Build the confusion matrix in counts, attach a cost or value to each cell, and include the capacity of the team consuming alerts.

Thresholds should be selected on validation data and then frozen for the held-out test. For ranking workflows with a fixed review budget, measure precision at the review capacity or lift over random selection. For probabilities used in pricing or prioritization, inspect **calibration**: among cases assigned 0.7, roughly 70% should occur. A model can rank well and still be badly calibrated.

Always segment metrics. An acceptable overall F1 can hide severe failure for a rare language, region, device, or customer class. Report support, the number of examples, beside every segment metric so tiny samples are not overinterpreted.
