You have already practised many of the skills assessed in an AI-engineering interview. Capstone defense questions, decision logs, evaluation reports, and project demonstrations all prepare you to explain technical choices under scrutiny. This lesson maps a typical interview process to the evidence you have built.

## The typical loop

1. **Screen**: "walk me through an AI project you built." Your portfolio case study, told in three minutes: problem, architecture, *measured* results, one honest failure. Practice it aloud; rambling here ends loops early.
2. **Coding**: standard software engineering plus AI-relevant tasks: parse malformed JSON safely, call an API with retries, or chunk documents. If an AI assistant is permitted, the interviewer may assess how carefully you review, test, and correct its output.
3. **System design**: the centerpiece: *"Design a support assistant over our internal docs"* or *"an invoice-processing pipeline."* This is Modules 8–16 as a conversation.
4. **Judgment/behavioral**: "tell me about a time the model was wrong in production," "how do you decide agent vs. workflow?" Your decision logs are the answer bank.

## Winning the system-design round

The differentiating move: **interviewers are testing for engineering judgment around the model, not model trivia.** A winning structure:

- **Clarify the job first** (Module 12's discipline): who uses it, what volume, what does an error cost? Thirty seconds of questions signals seniority instantly.
- **Draw the dependable system skeleton**: ingestion → retrieval or context assembly → model step with schema validation → confidence routing → human queue for consequential cases. Use deterministic code where the path is known and model judgment where ambiguity requires it.
- **State the quality and control plan**: explain that you would build a representative evaluation set before optimizing, and place an approval gate before consequential actions. Be prepared to justify the dataset, graders, threshold, and approval experience.
- **Volunteer the failure modes**: what happens when retrieval misses, the provider is down, an injection arrives in a document (Module 15). Naming risks unprompted is the strongest senior signal available.

## Handling what you don't know

You will be asked questions you cannot answer from memory. Respond by naming the gap and reasoning from stable principles: *"I have not used that vector database, but I would evaluate recall on our dataset, mandatory permission filters, index-update latency, operating cost, and recovery options."* An honest gap followed by a transferable evaluation framework is stronger than an unsupported claim. Interviewers will often probe the evidence behind each assertion.

## Preparation checklist

- Rehearse your three project narratives aloud (3 minutes each, ending on a measured result and an honest limitation).
- Redo the capstone oral-defense prompts (Module 17): they are the behavioral round.
- Do two mock system designs from cold: "AI triage for support tickets," "document pipeline for claims." Time-box 25 minutes, speak aloud, sketch.
- Review your evaluation results before the interview. Concrete numbers, such as a retrieval metric before and after a chunking change, make your project claims specific and credible.
