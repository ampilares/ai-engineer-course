Models don't read letters and don't read words. They read **tokens** (subword chunks from a fixed vocabulary) and immediately convert each one into a vector of numbers called an **embedding**. These two translation steps explain a surprising amount of LLM behavior, from API pricing to why models miscount the r's in "strawberry."

## Tokenization: text into chunks

A tokenizer is built (before training, via byte-pair encoding) by repeatedly merging the most frequent character pairs in a huge corpus until a vocabulary of ~50k–200k chunks exists. Common words become single tokens; rare words shatter into pieces. Try it:

<TokenizerExplorer />

Play with the samples and notice what falls out:

- **English prose** is cheap: a token per common word (~4 characters/token on average).
- **Code, rare words, and other languages** shatter into more tokens: the same information costs more.
- **Numbers** split oddly ("1234.56" is several tokens), which is one reason arithmetic is unnatural for LLMs.

The practical consequences are immediate: APIs price per token; context windows are measured in tokens; and the model literally cannot see inside a token: "strawberry" may be one opaque chunk, making "count the r's" a genuinely alien question.

## Embeddings: meaning as geometry

Each token ID indexes into a learned table to retrieve its **embedding**, typically a vector with hundreds or thousands of dimensions. Training shapes these vectors so that tokens used in related contexts often develop related representations. This is the first sense in which meaning becomes geometry, although contextual layers will change each token's representation based on the surrounding text.

<EmbeddingMap />

This one idea powers half the modern AI stack:

- Inside the model, embeddings are the working currency every layer transforms.
- Outside the model, **embedding entire sentences or documents** gives you semantic search: embed a query, embed your documents, and *nearest vectors = most related content*: even with zero words in common. That is the engine of retrieval-augmented generation in Module 9.

## Tokenization is an interface constraint

Token counts vary across languages, code, whitespace, and uncommon identifiers. A character limit is therefore not a reliable token limit. Count with the tokenizer for the exact model family when budgets matter, and reserve room for system instructions, tool schemas, retrieved context, conversation history, and output. Truncation should be an explicit policy, drop oldest turns, summarize, or reject, not an accidental slice through the most important evidence.

Embedding vectors depend on the model, version, and input convention. “Query” and “document” modes may be trained differently. Similarity scores are meaningful only within the same embedding space; re-embedding half an index with a new model silently corrupts retrieval. Store the embedding model/version beside every vector and rebuild the index atomically.

Cosine similarity measures geometric closeness, not truth, permission, causality, or recency. Two contradictory statements can be semantically close. Embeddings locate candidates; downstream filters and evidence checks decide whether they are usable.
