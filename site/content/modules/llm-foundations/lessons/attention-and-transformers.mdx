An initial embedding represents each token before the model has considered its surroundings. The meaning of a word such as "bank" depends on other tokens that distinguish a river bank from a savings bank. **Self-attention** is the mechanism that lets token representations exchange and combine contextual information.

## The problem attention solves

In *"The animal didn't cross the street because **it** was too tired"*, resolving "it" requires connecting it to "animal", seven tokens away. Older architectures passed information along token-by-token like a game of telephone, degrading over distance. Attention instead gives **every token a direct connection to every other token**, with learned, content-dependent strengths. See it:

<AttentionHeatmap />

Click "it" and observe how its attention concentrates on "animal." If the ending changed to "too wide," a trained model might instead attend more strongly to "street." **The routing depends on content as well as position.** Attention weights are learned signals, not guaranteed explanations of the model's decision.

## How the strengths are computed

Each token's embedding is projected into three vectors with learned weights:

- a **query**: "what am I looking for?"
- a **key**: "what do I offer?"
- a **value**: "what information do I carry?"

Each token's query is dotted with every token's key → similarity scores → softmax → weights that sum to 1. The token's new representation is the **weighted average of all the values**. In our example: "it"-the-pronoun has a query like *seeking a singular animate referent*; "animal"'s key matches strongly; so "it"'s updated vector absorbs "animal"'s content. After this layer, "it" effectively *means* "the tired animal."

One more mechanical fact matters for generation: a **causal mask** blocks each token from attending to tokens after it: you can't peek at the future you're trying to predict.

## The transformer block: attention plus thinking

A transformer is a stack of identical blocks, each doing two things:

1. **Attention**: tokens gather relevant context from each other (*communication*).
2. **MLP / feed-forward layer**: each token independently processes what it gathered (*computation*; this is also where much factual knowledge appears to live).

Wrapped in residual connections and normalization so gradients survive depth (Module 6's machinery), and with **many attention heads per layer** (each free to specialize in different relationships (syntax, coreference, rhyme…)) repeated dozens of times. After the final block, each position's vector is projected to a score per vocabulary token: the logits, one softmax away from next-token probabilities. The circle back to lesson 1 is complete.

## The engineering details behind the diagram

Self-attention needs position information because a set of token vectors alone has no order. Positional encodings or learned positional mechanisms let the network distinguish “dog bites person” from “person bites dog.” A **causal mask** prevents a decoder from attending to future tokens during next-token training. Multiple heads learn different projections, giving the layer several relationship patterns at once; their outputs are combined and passed through a feed-forward network, residual connections, and normalization.

The raw attention matrix grows roughly with sequence length squared in standard attention. Longer context therefore consumes memory and compute rapidly, even when the useful evidence is small. During autoregressive generation, a **key-value cache** reuses earlier attention computations instead of recalculating the whole prefix for every new token. That cache improves speed but grows with sequence length and concurrent users.

Attention weights can help inspect a toy mechanism, but they are not a complete explanation of model reasoning. Information is mixed across heads and layers, and high attention weight is not causal proof. Use behavior-based tests for claims about what the system relies on.
