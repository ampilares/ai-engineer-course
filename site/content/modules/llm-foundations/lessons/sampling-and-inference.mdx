The model hands you a probability distribution; **something still has to pick a token**. That choice (sampling) is where you, the engineer, hold real dials. Understanding them separates people who *use* model APIs from people who *control* them.

## The dials

- **Temperature** rescales logits before softmax. As temperature approaches zero, probability concentrates on the highest-scoring tokens and output becomes more repeatable. Around `1.0`, the distribution remains close to the model's learned shape. Higher values flatten the distribution, increasing variation and often increasing error. Low temperature does not make an answer factually safe; it only changes decoding.
- **Top-p (nucleus)** keeps only the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities sum to *p*, then renormalizes: a dynamic shortlist that adapts to the model's confidence.
- **Top-k**: same idea with a fixed-size shortlist.

## The context window: the model's entire world

The model conditions on at most N tokens: its **context window**. Everything it "knows" about your request must be inside: instructions, documents, conversation history. Outside the window = does not exist. Three engineering facts follow:

1. **Truncation is silent.** Overflow the window and something gets dropped; the model doesn't warn you it never saw page 40.
2. **Attention cost grows quadratically** with length: long contexts are slow and expensive.
3. **Long ≠ attentive.** Models recall the start and end of long contexts better than the middle (the "lost in the middle" effect): put crucial instructions at the edges, and don't stuff the window just because it fits. Module 9's retrieval exists largely to put *only the relevant* text in context.

## Inference mechanics worth knowing

- Generating N tokens = N sequential forward passes: why **output tokens dominate latency** and are priced several-fold above input tokens.
- The **KV cache** stores each token's keys/values so they aren't recomputed every step: why long prompts cost mostly at the start ("prefill"), and why providers can discount cached prompt prefixes. Structure your prompts static-part-first to exploit this (Module 8).

## Decoding is a product policy

Greedy decoding repeatedly selects the highest-probability token. It is stable but can produce brittle or repetitive text. Temperature rescales logits; top-*p* keeps the smallest set whose cumulative probability reaches a threshold. These controls interact, so change one at a time and evaluate on the actual task. Classification and extraction usually benefit from constrained outputs and low variability; brainstorming may benefit from a broader distribution.

“Temperature zero” is not a mathematical guarantee of identical responses across providers or hardware. Model revisions, routing, floating-point kernels, and ties can still change output. Treat a seed, when available, as a debugging aid rather than a correctness boundary. Deterministic correctness comes from validation, tests, and idempotent downstream code.

Latency has two parts: time to first token and time to finish. Streaming improves the former for a person watching, while shorter outputs, faster models, and parallelism change total completion time. Stop sequences and maximum-output limits need tests; a premature stop can produce an apparently successful but incomplete result.
