A network begins life with random weights and produces garbage. Training is the process that turns garbage into competence, and it rests on two ideas you can hold in one hand: a **loss function** that measures how wrong the network is, and **gradient descent** that tells every weight which way to move to be less wrong.

## Loss: wrongness as a single number

A loss function compares predictions with targets and returns a number that training tries to minimize. Mean squared error is common for regression. **Cross-entropy** is common for classification and penalizes confident mistakes more strongly than uncertain ones. Predicting a probability of `0.01` for the correct class produces a much larger loss than predicting `0.4`. Language-model pretraining applies the same principle to next-token prediction, which Module 7 develops further.

The loss defines *what the network cares about*. Choose it carelessly and the network will optimize precisely the wrong thing with superhuman diligence.

## Gradient descent: downhill in a million dimensions

Imagine the loss as a landscape where each horizontal direction is one weight and altitude is wrongness. Training wants the valley floor. Calculus provides the compass: the **gradient** (the direction of steepest ascent) computed for every weight at once. Step every weight a little *against* its gradient, and loss decreases:

```text
weight ← weight − learning_rate × gradient
```

Try it. The learning rate decides your step size, and it is the single most consequential dial in training:

## Backpropagation: assigning blame through layers

The gradient for a deep network's early weights depends on everything after them. **Backpropagation** computes all gradients efficiently by walking the chain rule backward from the loss: it answers, for every one of the millions of weights, *"how much did you contribute to this error?"*: in one backward pass costing about as much as the forward pass. This blame-assignment machinery is what makes training deep stacks feasible at all.

Two practical facts complete the picture:

- **Mini-batches:** gradients are estimated on small random batches (say 32–512 examples), not the full dataset: noisy but cheap, and the noise even helps escape bad spots. One full pass over the data is an **epoch**.
- **Optimizers:** plain gradient descent is rarely used raw. Adam and friends add per-weight step-size adaptation and momentum: think of them as power steering for the same downhill idea.

## A training step, made explicit

Every optimizer step follows the same order: load a mini-batch, run the forward pass, calculate loss, clear old gradients, backpropagate, update parameters, and record metrics. Accidentally retaining gradients across steps changes the update; applying activation and loss combinations incorrectly can make training numerically unstable. Prefer library losses that combine stable operations, such as cross-entropy applied to logits rather than hand-computing logarithms of softmax probabilities.

Gradients are also diagnostics. A norm near zero across early layers can mean vanishing gradients or a disconnected computation graph. Exploding norms often accompany unstable learning rates or long recurrent paths; gradient clipping can contain the symptom while you inspect the cause. Track loss and gradient norms on a short overfit test: a sufficiently expressive model should be able to memorize one tiny batch. If it cannot, suspect the data, labels, loss, or training loop before launching a long run.

## Guided build: make optimization fail on purpose

Then overfit a single mini-batch and verify that loss approaches zero. If it does not, debug the pipeline until it does. Your lab report must distinguish optimization failure, generalization failure, and an impossible task; those diagnoses lead to different fixes.
